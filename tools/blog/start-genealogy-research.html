How to Start Genealogy Research: A Beginner's Guide

Tracing your family history can feel like a big project. But every family tree starts the same way: one person writing down what they already know. This guide covers the first steps - which records to find, where to look for free, and the mistakes most beginners make.

Start with What You Already Know

Before you search any database, write down everything you know about your own family. Start with yourself and work backward: your parents, their parents, and as far as you can go. For each person, note their full name, birth date and place, marriage date, and death date. Gaps are normal - you will fill them in as you research.

Then talk to your relatives, especially the oldest ones. Ask about names, dates, places, and family stories. Stories are not always accurate, but they are full of clues. A story about "the uncle who came over from Italy" tells you to look for immigration records. Write down who told you each piece of information, because you will want to check it against records later.

The Records to Look For First

Genealogy runs on records - documents created during a person's life that prove who they were, where they lived, and who they were related to. These four types will do most of the heavy lifting when you are starting out:

  • Census records. Taken every ten years in the United States, census records list entire households: names, ages, birthplaces, and occupations. One census page can confirm a whole family in a single glance. U.S. census records are public through 1950.
  • Birth, marriage, and death certificates. Called "vital records," these are the most reliable documents for dates and parent names. Death certificates are especially useful because they often list the person's parents - pushing your tree back another generation.
  • Immigration and naturalization records. Ship passenger lists and citizenship papers show when an ancestor arrived, where they came from, and sometimes the town they left. These records are the bridge between your family in one country and its roots in another.
  • Military records. Draft cards, service files, and pension applications are packed with details: physical descriptions, addresses, next of kin. World War I and II draft cards alone cover a huge share of American men from those generations.

Work Backward, One Generation at a Time

Always research from the known to the unknown. Prove each generation with records before moving to the one before it. Skipping ahead is how people end up researching someone else's family by mistake.

Where to Find Records for Free

You do not need a paid subscription to make real progress. These free resources cover an enormous amount of ground:

  • FamilySearch (familysearch.org) - the largest free genealogy database in the world, run by a nonprofit. It has billions of searchable records, including census, vital, immigration, and military records. A free account unlocks everything.
  • The National Archives (archives.gov) - the official home of U.S. federal records, including census pages, military service files, and naturalization documents. Many are digitized and free to view.
  • State and county archives - vital records are usually kept at the state or county level. Many state archives have free online indexes of births, marriages, and deaths.
  • Your local library - many public libraries offer free in-library access to paid genealogy sites, plus local newspapers and city directories you will not find anywhere else.

How to Organize What You Find

If you do not stay organized, you will lose track of where your facts came from. Ten records in, you will not remember whether a date was from a census or a family story. A simple system fixes this:

  1. Record the source of every fact. For each date, name, or place, note which document it came from. "Grandma said so" and "1940 census, line 14" are very different levels of proof.
  2. Keep one page or file per person. List their key events - birth, marriage, moves, death - with the source for each. A timeline format works well; our free Timeline Builder lets you build one in your browser.
  3. Save copies of the records themselves. Download or photograph every document you find. Indexes contain errors; the original record is the proof. If a record is hard to read, our Record Analyzer can help you pull the details out of it.
  4. Write down the story as you go. Facts fade, but a written narrative keeps them meaningful. The Family History Writer can help you turn your findings into readable family stories.

Common Beginner Mistakes

Trusting other people's family trees. Online trees are full of copied errors. Treat them as hints, never as proof. Always find the underlying record.

Assuming names were spelled one way. Your ancestor "Schmidt" may appear as Smith, Smitt, or Schmitt across different records. Spelling was flexible, and census takers wrote what they heard. Search for variations.

Skipping generations. Jumping from yourself straight to a famous ancestor with the same last name almost never holds up. Build the chain one proven link at a time.

Ignoring siblings and cousins. Your direct ancestor's records may be lost, but their brother's death certificate might name the same parents. Researching the whole family solves brick walls that a straight-line search cannot. If you are unsure how a relative connects to you, our guide on how cousin relationships work makes it simple.

Not writing down where facts came from. The most painful mistake of all - because the only fix is redoing the research.

Your First Week, Step by Step

  1. Write down everything you know about your parents and grandparents.
  2. Interview your oldest living relatives and take notes.
  3. Create a free FamilySearch account and find your family in the most recent public census.
  4. Order or locate birth and death certificates for your grandparents.
  5. Organize each person's facts into a timeline with sources. Our guide to building a family history timeline shows you how.

That is genuinely all it takes to begin. Family history research rewards patience over speed - each record you find and verify becomes solid ground for the next generation back.
