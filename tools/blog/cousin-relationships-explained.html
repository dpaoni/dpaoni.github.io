How Cousin Relationships Work: A Simple Guide

If you have ever looked at a family tree and wondered what "second cousin once removed" actually means, you are not alone. Cousin relationships sound complicated, but they follow a simple system. Once you understand the two key rules, you can figure out any family relationship in seconds.

The Two Rules of Cousin Relationships

Every cousin relationship is based on two things:

  1. How many generations back is your shared ancestor? This gives you the "number" — first cousin, second cousin, third cousin, and so on.
  2. Are you in the same generation as your cousin? If not, the difference tells you how many times "removed" you are.

That is it. Those two numbers give you the full relationship name. Here is how it works step by step.

Step 1: Find the Shared Ancestor

Start by finding the person you and your cousin both descend from. This is your shared ancestor. For most cousins, this will be a grandparent or great-grandparent.

Count the number of generations between you and that shared ancestor:

  • If the shared ancestor is your parent, that is 1 generation.
  • If the shared ancestor is your grandparent, that is 2 generations.
  • If the shared ancestor is your great-grandparent, that is 3 generations.

Do the same count for your cousin. Take the smaller of the two numbers and subtract 1. That gives you the cousin number:

  • 2 generations back, minus 1 = first cousins (share grandparents)
  • 3 generations back, minus 1 = second cousins (share great-grandparents)
  • 4 generations back, minus 1 = third cousins (share great-great-grandparents)

Step 2: Check the Generation Gap

If you and your cousin are the same number of generations from the shared ancestor, you are just regular cousins — no "removed" needed. For example, if your grandparent is also your cousin's grandparent, you are first cousins. Simple.

But what if you are different distances from the shared ancestor? That is where "removed" comes in.

The "removed" number is simply the difference between your generations. If you are 2 generations from the ancestor and your cousin is 3 generations away, the difference is 1 — so you are first cousins once removed.

Quick Reference

Same grandparents = First cousins
Same great-grandparents = Second cousins
Same great-great-grandparents = Third cousins
One generation apart = Once removed
Two generations apart = Twice removed

Common Examples

Your parent's first cousin is your... first cousin once removed. Your parent is 2 generations from the shared grandparent. The cousin is also 2 generations from that grandparent — making them first cousins to each other. But you are 3 generations from that same grandparent. The 1-generation difference makes them your first cousin once removed.

Your grandparent's first cousin is your... first cousin twice removed. They are 2 generations from their shared ancestor. You are 4 generations from that same ancestor. The 2-generation gap means twice removed.

Your sibling is your... not a cousin at all. Siblings share parents (1 generation back). The cousin formula gives zero — which is the special case for siblings.

The Aunt and Uncle Exception

If one person is only 1 generation from the shared ancestor (they are the child of that ancestor) and the other person is further away, the relationship is aunt/uncle, not a cousin. For example:

  • Your parent's sibling = your aunt or uncle
  • Your grandparent's sibling = your great-aunt or great-uncle

This only happens when the person closer to the ancestor is exactly 1 generation away.
